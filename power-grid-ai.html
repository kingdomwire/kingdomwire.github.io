The Load Forecast

Half the national grid is being scaled up at once, the state’s power buyer told the energy congress in Riyadh, with a 2030 deadline to deliver what industry and AI will demand. The constraint on the Kingdom’s compute ambitions is electrical, and the state is treating it that way.

Energy13 October 2026·4 min read·KingdomWire

The most consequential number spoken at the WPC Energy Congress on Tuesday was not an oil figure. “About 50 percent of the grid is being scaled up today,” Mazin Al-Bahkali, chief executive of the Saudi Power Procurement Co., told a session on energy and artificial intelligence in Riyadh. Grids are usually upgraded at the margin, a substation and a transmission line at a time. Rebuilding half of one simultaneously is what a country does when it expects demand to arrive in blocks rather than increments.

The blocks have names. Al-Bahkali listed industry first and AI second, and the second item is the one arriving in blocks: a single hyperscale data center can draw as much power as a mid-sized industrial city, and the Kingdom has contracted for several. The Microsoft Azure and AWS cloud regions go live over the coming months. HUMAIN’s build program has placed its first work orders, 50 megawatts in the opening tranche of an agreement that runs to 250. Each of those megawatts has to exist as generation, transmission and settled price before it can exist as compute.

Al-Bahkali’s company, known in the sector as the Principal Buyer, is the instrument the state has chosen for that problem. The government is drafting an energy mix designed to keep supply dependable and competitively priced, he said, and “Principal Buyer is being tasked to execute on that plan to be able to deliver that power by 2030.” The procurement model matters. Rather than letting each large consumer contract its own supply, the Kingdom routes demand through a single buyer that forecasts consumption, tenders capacity and signs the long-term agreements that make private capital willing to build. The Rabigh 2 gas plant that closed its 9.69 billion riyal financing this month on a roughly 34-year structure is what that model produces: generation underwritten for decades against demand the state has already aggregated.

The forecasting itself is becoming an AI exercise. Alanoud Al-Shuraym, general manager of enterprise architecture at the Ministry of Energy, described systems that pull live data from across the energy value chain into the ministry’s monitoring center, down to fuel stations, and simulate incidents before they occur to assess how a disruption would propagate. The ministry works with HUMAIN and the Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority among others. The circularity is deliberate: AI is both the demand the grid must serve and the tool the ministry uses to plan the serving. Margarete Schramboeck, a board member at Aramco Digital, compressed the point at the same session: “It’s two sides of one coin, and we cannot do one without the other.”

The state’s own ledger shows how much weight the digital side already carries. Government spending on information and communications technology has reached almost 32 billion riyals, Anas Al-Rimi of the Digital Government Authority told the congress, with spending on emerging AI technologies up 20 percent and more than 9.5 billion riyals in measured benefit to the wider economy. Seven framework agreements now cover over 60 billion riyals of spending across more than 100 providers, and the authority plans to reach 2,400 government AI services by the end of this year. Those services run on the same grid the Principal Buyer is scaling.

The 2030 deadline aligns three calendars that previously ran separately: the generation pipeline, the data-center commitments and the Vision 2030 reform program that reorganized the power sector into buyer, producers and regulator in the first place. The alignment is the story. Countries that treat electricity as an afterthought of their AI strategies discover the lead times too late; turbines and transmission corridors take longer to build than server halls.

What to watch is the tender flow. Half a grid implies a procurement schedule, and the Principal Buyer’s next capacity rounds, gas and renewable alike, will show whether the 2030 commitment is tracking. The interconnection memorandum signed with Kuwait this week points the same direction: a grid built for block demand is also a grid with surplus worth exporting.
