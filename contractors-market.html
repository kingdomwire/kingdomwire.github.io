Open Door, Local Rules

The Saudi Contractors Authority counts 1,648 foreign contracting firms in the market and more than SR3 trillion of projects coming over three years, while procurement rules push local content past half of government spending. The two moves are one policy: import capacity, keep the value at home.

Construction12 August 2026·4 min read·KingdomWire

The Saudi Contractors Authority counts 1,648 foreign contracting companies that have entered the Kingdom since the authority was established, on a register that now holds 140,000 local and foreign firms, and it expects more than SR3 trillion ($800 billion) of projects to reach the market over the next three years. Chairman Mohammed Al-Ajlan's summary, reported this week by Al-Eqtisadiah, is that the Kingdom has become a destination for contractors as much as for capital. The number that explains why is the simplest one: contracting now contributes roughly 8 percent of Saudi GDP, and the order book ahead of it is larger than any domestic industry could deliver alone.

The composition of last year's work says where the pressure sits. Of 472 projects launched, building and construction took the largest count, 270 projects worth SR74 billion. But water and energy, with 84 projects worth SR79 billion, took the largest value. An average water or energy award ran close to a billion riyals, more than three times the typical building contract. Those are the packages, desalination, transmission, generation, district cooling, that demand specialized engineering capacity in scarce global supply. The foreign firms the authority is registering are not mainly there to pour concrete; they are there for the categories Saudi demand has outgrown.

Al-Ajlan has been careful about what entry means. Foreign contractors receive no special treatment, he told CNBC Arabia, and the roughly 18,000 classified Saudi firms should treat them as partners in knowledge exchange rather than rivals. That framing is aimed at the domestic industry, which the authority describes as the executive arm of Vision 2030, and it matches how the wider procurement system is being tuned.

The Other Half of the Policy

While the contractors' door opens, the Local Content and Government Procurement Authority is hardening the rules about where project value lands. In the second quarter alone, 8,600 government tenders worth SR63 billion were issued under local content mechanisms, and the mandatory list of nationally produced goods grew by 79 items to 1,752. Local content's share of government procurement has climbed from 28 percent in 2018 to above half last year. Read together, the two policies are one policy: import construction capacity at the scale the pipeline requires, and use procurement rules to keep an increasing share of the spending inside the Kingdom's own supply chains.

The frictions are the familiar ones for a market growing this fast. Al-Ajlan acknowledged that financing constraints and payment cycles remain the sector's working problems, and said the authority is engaging government entities and banks to shorten them. For an international contractor pricing Saudi work, the SR3 trillion pipeline is the draw and the payment cycle is the discount rate. How quickly invoices clear will do more to determine which firms commit engineering teams to Riyadh than any licensing reform.

The demand side is not in question. Expo 2030 in Riyadh, the 2034 FIFA World Cup, the giga-projects and the utility build-out behind them give the sector what construction markets almost never have: a decade of committed public demand with dates attached. That certainty is the recruiting pitch, and 1,648 foreign entrants suggest it is being heard.

The figures worth following from here are the cadence ones: whether foreign registrations keep compounding as stadium and Expo packages tender, whether the authority's qualification programs move smaller Saudi firms into the billion-riyal categories now dominated by international groups, and how far the mandatory products list extends before the localization curve begins to flatten.
