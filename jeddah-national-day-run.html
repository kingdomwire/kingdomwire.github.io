The Long Run

Six thousand runners crossed Historic Jeddah at dawn on Friday in the Green Spirit Run, three times the field the National Day race drew two years ago. Mass participation is the quieter half of Saudi sports policy, and the half with the longer payoff.

Sport19 September 2026·3 min read·KingdomWire

The most useful number from Friday’s dawn race through Historic Jeddah is the field, not a finishing time: 6,000 runners on the 5 and 2 kilometer courses through Al-Balad’s alleys, against 2,000 when the event ran for National Day two years ago. A race field that trebles across two editions is measuring something beyond the event itself, which is the habit of ordinary exercise taking hold.

The Green Spirit Run, now a fixture of National Day week, was organized by the Masafat community running club with the Ministry of Sport, the Sports for All Federation and the Jeddah Historic District program. The 5 kilometer course took professionals and serious amateurs; the 2 kilometer route took families and children. Khalid Yamani, the club’s president, said the race aims to build community awareness of physical fitness while reinforcing national belonging, a fair summary of the double duty the event performs four days before National Day on September 23.

Saudi sports policy runs on two tracks that are easy to conflate. The visible track acquires: leagues, star signings, global tournaments, a World Cup in 2034. The quieter track participates: federation-backed community clubs, school sport, subsidized races whose entire purpose is to get residents moving. The second track is where the fiscal logic lives. A population that exercises costs its health system less, and the Quality of Life Program has treated regular physical activity as a national target rather than a private virtue since 2018. Community clubs like Masafat are the delivery mechanism: the state sets the calendar and lends the venue, and the clubs recruit the runners. Races like this one are deliberately cheap to enter and short enough for a first-timer to finish, the design features of a habit rather than a spectacle.

The venue is doing policy work of its own. Al-Balad, a UNESCO World Heritage site in the middle of a long restoration program, could have been preserved as scenery and viewed from behind rope lines. Using it as a race course, a festival ground and an ordinary civic space instead reflects a specific theory of heritage: districts stay alive by hosting the city’s routines, not by being protected from them. A dawn field of 6,000 moving through coral-stone streets is the theory working.

The field itself told a Jeddah story. Runners included Saudis and expatriates of many nationalities, Filipino residents prominent among them, in a port city whose composition has always been layered. A National Day race open to everyone who lives in the Kingdom reads the holiday as a residents’ occasion, consistent with how the quality-of-life buildout has been widening the audience for national events beyond citizens and beyond spectators.

National Day itself lands on Tuesday, with the usual concentration of ceremony. The more telling indicators come afterward: whether the winter calendar carries participation events onto Diriyah, AlUla and the other heritage stages now coming online, and what next year’s field looks like. Sports economies are usually judged by what they can host. This one is starting to be judged by how many people show up to move.
