The Nuclear Deal Acquires a Political Price

Two days after Washington and Riyadh announced a 30-year civil nuclear agreement, President Trump made it conditional on Saudi membership in the Abraham Accords. A technical file is now the region's largest political negotiation.

News25 July 2026·4 min read·KingdomWire

The most consequential clause of the US-Saudi nuclear agreement was attached after the signing. On Wednesday, US Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman announced a 30-year civil nuclear cooperation deal, the product of years of negotiation over reactors, fuel and safeguards. On Thursday, President Trump declared the agreement "totally subject to Saudi Arabia joining the very respected and successful Abraham Accords." Neither government's announcement had mentioned Israel. One post moved the file from the technical track it had traveled for two years onto the one regional negotiation that has resisted resolution.

The confirmed terms first. The agreement runs 30 years and anchors American firms in the Saudi civil nuclear program. The question that stalled talks for years, enrichment on Saudi soil, was deferred rather than resolved: a joint study, expected to take up to two years, will determine whether an enrichment facility is permitted, and any such plant would be operated by US companies using controlled technology. Until then the Kingdom would import low-enriched fuel. Saudi Arabia has not adopted the International Atomic Energy Agency's additional protocol for expanded monitoring, and the deal requires congressional review.

The Post and the Text

Trump's post described the arrangement as pertaining "only to non-military use such as the ones that Iran and UAE (and others) already have," with no enrichment of material. The announced structure and the post do not fully match. The UAE renounced enrichment outright in 2009; the Saudi text leaves the question open to the study. The distance between the two descriptions is where the next phase of negotiation will happen, and each party now has a version to point to.

The condition has an author beyond the White House. Israel's Channel 12 reported that Israeli officials lobbied intensively in the day between the announcement and Trump's post to have normalization attached retroactively. Prime Minister Netanyahu's office welcomed the outcome, calling Saudi accession to the accords a "historic leap forward for peace in the Middle East." White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said the president is the "final dealmaker" and has repeatedly urged Riyadh to join.

Silence as a Position

Riyadh has said nothing officially; the Saudi embassy in Washington declined comment. A Saudi source quoted by the journalist Laura Rozen was blunter: "It was signed. So nothing to renegotiate. Tweets don't overturn signed agreements." The Kingdom's stated terms for normalization have not changed since 2023: a credible path to Palestinian statehood, which the current Israeli government rejects. Treating the text as settled costs Riyadh nothing and obliges Washington to decide whether a signed agreement is, in fact, subject to a post.

The timing explains the leverage on both sides. The agreement was signed in the middle of a war in which American forces defend Saudi airspace and American policy underwrites the security of Saudi exports. Washington is converting that dependence into diplomatic yield while the dependence is at its maximum, and Israel is converting its standing in Washington into a veto it does not formally hold. Conditions attached after signature carry a cost of their own, though. They price American commitments as revisable, which is the doubt Gulf capitals have spent a decade hedging against, and they set a public valuation on normalization. Riyadh now knows what Washington will pay for it, and can hold the asset while the price rises.

Where the Condition Becomes Law

Congress is the venue where the condition could become more than a post. An agreement of this type must survive review, and skepticism about Saudi enrichment runs through both parties; a 2018 proposal requiring affirmative congressional approval for any Saudi nuclear deal was reintroduced in March. Steven Cook of the Council on Foreign Relations described the file as sitting in "a state of diplomatic suspension." The suspension has a structure: the text exists, the signature exists, and approval now depends on a negotiation with a third government that was not at the signing table.

The agreement was presented as a 30-year commitment. Its first week measured the two clocks it actually runs on: the technical one written into the text, and the political one that resets each morning in Washington. Which clock governs will be visible soon, in whether the joint study begins on schedule, in whether Riyadh keeps its silence, and in whether Congress treats the condition as policy or improvisation.
